# Gobos and projection attachments: what to imitate (researched 2026-10-15)

The question: what real projection attachments ship with, what the classic stock gobo catalogues contain, and which
physical effects make a projected pattern read as real light. This feeds projmap's "static mode", where the projector
acts as a spotlight with a gobo. Every claim carries a source number (see **Sources** at the end). "Unverified" means
no retailer or manufacturer page we could read confirmed the detail.

## 1. Standard gobo sizes

Rosco's size table [R1] is the reference most attachment makers follow:

| size | outside diameter | image area | typical fixture |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 100 mm | 75 mm | large ellipsoidals, Source Four |
| B | 86 mm | 64.5 mm | the industry standard for ellipsoidals |
| M | 66 mm | 48 mm | Jr. ellipsoidals, compact LED projection mounts |

Godox quotes its M-size gobos as 66 mm OD with a 50 mm image area [G2], slightly larger than Rosco's 48 mm. The image
area, not the outside diameter, sets the projected pattern size [G2]. Wikipedia lists sizes from C (150 mm) down to
E (37.5 mm) and moving-head sizes [W2].

## 6. Physics that sell the look

### Focus and soft edge

An ellipsoidal (and every attachment above) puts the gobo and shutters at the gate, a focal plane of the optics; the
lens tube slides to make the edge hard or soft [W1][W3]. Every attachment above focuses by sliding the lens [A9][A11][A14].
A real "window light" is usually run slightly soft so mullions read as light, not as graphic lines (lighting practice;
the softness itself is a defocus blur). The softness should scale with how far the surface sits from the focal
distance: Wikipedia's keystone article notes a tilted surface cannot be in focus everywhere because the lens focuses
at one distance [W4]. So a raked wall is sharp in one band and soft toward its ends.

### Low sun stretch and keystone

When the projection hits a surface at an angle, the pattern stretches into a trapezoid (keystone) [W4]. For sunlight
the same geometry applies: a shadow on a floor has length h / tan(elevation), so a sun at 15° throws a shadow about
3.7x the window height, and at 60° about 0.58x (plain trigonometry, no source needed). Long, raked, keystoned panes are
what make "late afternoon" read.

### Chromatic fringe

Lateral chromatic aberration focuses each wavelength at a different position across the image plane, so high-contrast
edges toward the frame border get red and blue fringes; stopping down does not cure it, and achromatic doublets reduce
it [W5]. Aputure markets "minimal edge fringing" on its premium optic [A8], which implies cheaper single-element lenses
show it. A small radial red/blue offset at the pattern edge imitates this.

### Vignetting and hot spot

Natural vignetting falls off roughly as cos⁴ of the field angle; optical vignetting depends on aperture [W6]. Projection
attachments advertise "even" beams [A8][A12], so a soft falloff toward the edge of the circle is the realistic default
for cheaper units, and a flat field is the "premium" setting. Field angle is defined at 10% of centre intensity [W3].

### Penumbra from a large source (frame depth)

A shadow from an extended source has an umbra and a penumbra, the region where only part of the source is hidden [W7].
Penumbra width is roughly source angular size times the distance from the occluder to the surface [P1]. The sun spans
about 0.5° [P1], so each metre between a window frame and the wall adds about 9 mm of soft edge. A deep window reveal
has two occluding edges (inner and outer face of the frame) at different distances, which gives the doubled,
graded edge seen in real window light. This is a geometric consequence of [W7][P1]; we found no single source that
states the "double edge" directly.

### Colour temperature of the sun

Values vary with source and atmosphere; the commonly quoted photography ranges are:

| sun | approx. CCT | source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| at the horizon (sunrise, sunset) | 1850 K to 3000 K | [W8] (1850 K), [P2] (2000 to 3000 K) |
| golden hour (0° to 6° elevation) | 2500 K to 3500 K | [P2][P3] |
| midday, 30°+ elevation | 5500 K to 6500 K | [P2]; Wikipedia gives 5500 to 6000 K for "vertical daylight" [W8] |
| sun above the atmosphere | about 5900 K | [P4] |
| blue hour (sun 4° to 8° below) | 9000 K to 12000 K | [P2] |

The brief's "3200 to 3500 K golden hour" sits inside these ranges.

### Haze and dust

A beam becomes visible when particles scatter part of it sideways toward the viewer (the Tyndall effect); sun shafts
through foliage in mist are the natural case, and theatres add haze for the same reason [P5][W9]. On a projector this
means a faint, soft glow that follows the pattern, brighter toward the source and stronger in the open panes.

## 7. What projmap replicates

Since catalog 6 the library is only the plates of section 8: the designs on the user's reference photos, nothing
invented. The shader-drawn attachments of catalog 4 (windows, blinds, leaves, patterns and spots, with the real
analogues researched above) and the 23 original plates of catalog 5 were removed; stored scenes move to the closest
plate or window preset (protocol §16, Migration). The windows, blinds and fine lines are one parametric window, so
panes, mullions, arches, perspective, diamond lattices and slats stay adjustable as the shader windows were.

Parameters every attachment exposes:

- **rotation**: real holders rotate (Spotlight Max geared holder, Spotlight Mini 360° barrel [A9][A11]).
- **scale**: stands in for lens angle (19° / 26° / 36° / 50°, or the 15° to 30° zoom) and throw distance.
- **focus / softness**: lens-slide defocus, plus a depth-dependent term for raked surfaces [W1][W4].
- **throw and keystone skew**: sun elevation and azimuth, giving stretch and trapezoid [W4].
- **chromatic fringe**: radial red/blue offset at edges, 0 for "premium optic" [W5][A8].
- **vignette**: cos⁴-style falloff toward the circle edge, 0 for flat field [W6].
- **haze**: visible glow following the open areas [P5].
- **colour**: CCT presets from the sun table (sunrise 2000 K, golden hour 3200 K, noon 5600 K, blue hour 10000 K).
- **frame depth**: second, wider penumbra edge per [W7][P1].

## 8. The plate library (catalog 6)

The references are the user's photos in `design/refs/gobo-plates/` (its README says which part of each photo is
light): the amaran AS-GB-M SET1 and SET2 M-size metal plates (MO05 to MO24), a six-plate set (set A), three close-up
sets (set 2, set 3, set 4 windows) and a 15-plate paper set (set B). Several designs repeat across sheets; each design
appears once and its `source` names every sheet it is on (the sunburst is MO05, the set A sunburst and set B's
radiant light; the scattered sticks are MO13 and set A's; the slit is set 3's line slit and set B's gap). Similar but
different designs stay separate: the set A flames (a band of slender tongues, "bonfire") are not MO19 (big licking
tongues), the set A breakup (big ragged patches, "leaf breakup") is not MO23 (small shards), set 2's breakup is
small rounded blobs. Set 4's grid window shows 5 × 5 panes although the README calls it 4 × 6; the preset follows the
photo.

M-size plates have about a 48 to 50 mm image area [R1][G2]; the plates draw that area as a round field of radius
about 0.84 of the plate, so 1 plate unit is about 29 mm and the 0.012 minimum opening and bridge width is about
0.35 mm (window bars 0.006, about 0.17 mm, as on etched window gobos), within what laser-cut or etched steel holds.
Steel needs bridges for islands [W2], so every design ties its islands to the rim: woven rings break where one passes
under another, the swirl's slots run between curved bridges from the centre to the rim, the rainbow arch's bands end
at the field edge so the metal between them stays tied, maze pieces sit in a metal web, the inner squares of window
grid III hang on the four lattice bars that meet there. Scattered designs keep their openings apart (a merged ring of
openings would drop a piece of metal), branches and cracks are placed so no two strokes cross (they always form a
tree), and leaves keep clear of branches.

The renderer projects the round field by default (the spotlight's aperture); `field: fill | stretch | tile` makes a
plate cover a 16:9 frame like a wide projector.

The windows (category windows, `family: "window"`) are one generator (`frontend/src/render/plates/windows.ts`):
sashes of panes, a frame and mullions, an arch head with spokes and a hub, a round outline, a perspective homography,
diamond lattices with inner squares, and slats; each reference window is a preset of it (protocol §16 lists the
params and presets).
